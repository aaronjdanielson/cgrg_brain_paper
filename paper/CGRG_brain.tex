\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We propose a model for directed, real-valued networks in which the dependence
between the two directed edges within each dyad is captured by a one-parameter
Frank copula selected on the basis of empirical dependence structure and
predictive likelihood, while the marginal mean of each edge is decomposed into
additive sender and receiver effects.  The model is closed-form, tractable under No-U-Turn Sampling (NUTS) via NumPyro and JAX, and designed
for replicated networks (multiple
networks observed on a common node set, each belonging to one of $G$ comparison
groups).

We derive several theoretical properties that clarify the interpretation and
estimability of the dyadic reciprocity parameter.  The copula parameter $\rho$
admits a closed-form, margin-invariant interpretation as a monotone function of
Kendall's $\tau$ via the Debye function.  Under a standard normalisation of the
sender effects, the full parameter vector is identifiable whenever $N \geq 3$.
With fixed $N$ and growing number of replicated networks $S$, the MLE of $\rho$
is $\sqrt{S}$-consistent and asymptotically normal under a simplified model
serving as a parametric benchmark.

The model is applied to effective connectivity networks in the default mode
network from resting-state fMRI for 112 participants across three diagnostic
groups: Alzheimer's disease, mild cognitive impairment, and cognitively normal.
Posterior estimates of $\rho$ are strongly negative for all three groups
($\rho^{(g)} \approx -3.7$ to $-3.8$, 95\% credible intervals well below zero),
indicating that within each dyad, the two directed edges are negatively
associated: participants with a stronger effective connection $A_{ij}$ tend to
have a weaker reverse connection $A_{ji}$, consistent with a hierarchical
regulation structure in the DMN.  The three groups have nearly identical
posterior distributions for $\rho$; the posterior probability of the strict
ordering $\rho^{(\mathrm{NL})} < \rho^{(\mathrm{MCI})} < \rho^{(\mathrm{AD})}$
is $0.20$, close to the uniform chance level of $1/6$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Directed weighted networks, in which each ordered pair of nodes carries a
real-valued edge weight, arise in neuroscience, economics, and the social
sciences.  In neuroscience, dynamic causal modelling (DCM; \citealp{friston2003,
friston2014}) converts resting-state fMRI time series into an $N \times N$
matrix of effective connectivity strengths, one matrix per participant.  The
analyst then faces the problem of comparing the distributional properties of
these connectivity matrices across diagnostic groups, such as Alzheimer's disease (AD),
mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and cognitively normal (NL) individuals
\citep{nie2020, huang2022, monash2024}.

A natural feature of such networks is \emph{reciprocity}: the extent to which
a strong directed edge $A_{ij}$ tends to co-occur with a strong (or weak)
reverse edge $A_{ji}$.  Existing frameworks handle reciprocity in several
ways.  The $p_1$ model \citep{holland81} and its extensions parametrize
reciprocity via an explicit differential parameter.  The additive and
multiplicative effects (AME) model \citep{hoff2005} captures reciprocity
implicitly through multiplicative latent factors.  The recent model of
\citet{fengleng2025} establishes consistency results for a directed network
model with explicit sender/receiver effects and a reciprocity term.  For
real-valued edges specifically, \citet{zhang2020} develop a mixed-effects
time-varying network model, and \citet{leekimbrain2019} apply copula-based
directional dependence to whole-brain connectivity from fMRI.

Unlike most network models that study a single large network growing in its
number of nodes, our setting consists of many replicated networks on a small
fixed node set.  This regime arises naturally in neuroscience, where each
participant contributes one network over a fixed set of brain regions, and
motivates an asymptotic theory in which the number of replicates $S$ grows
while $N$ is fixed.

This paper proposes a model for \emph{replicated} directed real-valued
networks that makes reciprocity both structurally explicit and statistically
interpretable.  The key idea is to model the two directed edges of each
\emph{dyad} (the unordered pair $\{i,j\}$) jointly via a bivariate Frank
copula, while specifying the marginal distribution of each edge through
additive sender and receiver node effects.  Because dyads are treated as
conditionally independent of one another, the joint likelihood factorises
over dyads, and the Frank copula parameter $\rho$ becomes the sole driver of
within-dyad dependence.  We call this model a \emph{Copula Generated Random
Graph} (CGRG).

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A parsimonious model for directed real-valued networks combining an
  interpretable additive mean structure with a single reciprocity parameter
  (Section~\ref{sec:realvalued}).
\item A Bayesian hierarchical extension that pools information across $G$
  comparison groups through shared hyperparameters on the sender effects,
  receiver effects, and reciprocity parameters
  (Section~\ref{sec:cgrg_brain}).
\item Several theoretical results (Section~\ref{sec:theory}): a closed-form,
  margin-invariant Kendall's $\tau$ formula, identifiability for $N \geq 3$,
  and a parametric benchmark establishing $\sqrt{S}$-consistency of the MLE
  under fixed $N$ and growing replicates $S$.  (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_entropy}
  provides an entropy decomposition that further clarifies the role of $\rho$
  but is not needed for the empirical results.)
\item A proof-of-concept application to $N = 4$ default mode network
  connectivity matrices from 112 fMRI participants across AD, MCI, and NL
  groups (Section~\ref{sec:brain_app}).  The model recovers strongly negative
  reciprocity ($\rho \approx -3.75$, Kendall's $\tau \approx -0.43$) for
  all three groups with 95\% credible intervals entirely below zero,
  providing evidence of stable hierarchical DMN organisation across disease
  states in this cohort.  Posterior predictive checks confirm that the model
  reproduces the observed dyadic Kendall's $\tau$ pattern.
\end{enumerate}

Several important limitations of the current framework should be noted from
the outset.  First, the model assumes \emph{dyadic independence}: the joint
distribution of $A_{ij}$ and $A_{ji}$ is modelled through a bivariate copula,
but dependence among different dyads---such as transitivity or triadic
closure---is excluded.  This is a deliberate simplification that ensures
tractability and identifiability in the small-$N$ replicated setting but
rules out higher-order network phenomena.  Second, the theoretical results
in Section~\ref{sec:theory} are derived under a simplified model with known
marginal parameters; the full hierarchical model lacks corresponding asymptotic
theory, and extending identifiability and consistency to the hierarchical
setting remains an open problem.  Third, the brain application with 112
participants (only $S_{\mathrm{AD}} = 12$ in the AD group) should be
understood as a proof-of-concept illustration rather than definitive
neuroscience validation; stronger conclusions about disease-related differences
would require larger, externally validated cohorts.

Section~\ref{sec:cgrg} introduces the dyad-separable framework.
Section~\ref{sec:realvalued} specifies the Gaussian-marginal Frank-copula
model.  Section~\ref{sec:theory} contains the core theoretical results
(Kendall's $\tau$, identifiability, and asymptotic consistency).
Section~\ref{sec:cgrg_brain} develops the hierarchical multi-group model
for brain networks.  Section~\ref{sec:compare} compares the CGRG to a

Section~\ref{sec:brain_app} contains the brain application.
Section~\ref{sec:discuss} discusses extensions and concludes.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_entropy} contains the entropy decomposition;
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_plots} contains posterior density plots for node

\section{Dyad-Separable Copula Generated Random Graphs}
\label{sec:cgrg}

Let $G$ be a directed network on $N$ nodes with adjacency matrix $A$.  The
key structural assumption of a copula generated random graph is that the
$N(N-1)/2$ \emph{dyads} (unordered pairs $\{i,j\}$, each comprising the
two directed edges $A_{ij}$ and $A_{ji}$) are conditionally independent of
one another.  Within each dyad, the joint distribution of $(A_{ij}, A_{ji})$
is specified via a copula binding their marginal CDFs.  Formally, for any
$\{i,j\}$ and $\{k,l\}$ with $\{i,j\} \neq \{k,l\}$,

\[
(A_{ij}, A_{ji}) \perp (A_{kl}, A_{lk}) \mid \theta, \rho,
\]

so the log-likelihood factorises as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:factor}
\log p(A \mid \theta, \rho)
  = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p(A_{ii} \mid \theta)
  + \sum_{i < j} \log p(A_{ij}, A_{ji} \mid \theta, \rho).
\end{equation}

This dyadic separability plays the same structural role that block-diagonal
dependence plays in stochastic block models: it makes inference tractable and
gives each marginal and copula parameter a direct interpretation.  The
parameter $\rho$ governs \emph{reciprocity}, the tendency of $A_{ij}$ and
$A_{ji}$ to be concordant or discordant, and is common to all dyads within
a group.

The assumption restricts dependence to occur only within unordered pairs of
nodes.  It excludes higher-order dependencies such as transitivity, triadic
closure, or correlated sender and receiver effects across distinct dyads.
The dyadic independence assumption is not intended to capture all forms of
network dependence; rather, it isolates reciprocity as the dominant
dependence mechanism while keeping inference tractable in replicated-network
settings.
The assumption is deliberate: it yields a likelihood that factorises over
dyads, reducing computation to a sum over $N(N-1)/2$ independent terms and
isolating reciprocity as the primary dependence mechanism.  In the
replicated-network regime with small fixed $N$ (the setting of the brain
application in Section~\ref{sec:cgrg_brain}), dyadic separability provides
a parsimonious alternative to
high-dimensional covariance models that become inestimable when the number of
replicates is modest relative to $N^4$.

\section{A Dyadic Copula Model for Real-Valued Networks}
\label{sec:realvalued}

Many networks arising in the natural and social sciences carry real-valued
edge weights.  Regulatory strength between brain regions, trade volumes
between countries, and communication frequencies between colleagues are
all examples.  This section develops a CGRG model for directed networks
with real-valued edges within the dyad-separable framework of
Section~\ref{sec:cgrg}.

\subsection{Model Specification}

Let $G$ be a directed network on $N$ nodes with edge weight matrix
$A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$.  Following the block-separable construction
of Section~\ref{sec:cgrg}, we partition the off-diagonal edges into $M$
groups such that edges belonging to different groups are conditionally
independent.  We take the dyadic partition, so $M = N(N-1)/2$ and each
group contains the two directed edges $A_{ij}$ and $A_{ji}$ connecting the
same pair of nodes.  Under this partition the log-likelihood decomposes as
\begin{align}
\label{eq:real_loglik}
\log p(A \mid \theta, \rho)
  = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p(A_{ii} \mid \theta)
  + \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} \sum_{j > i}
    \log p(A_{ij}, A_{ji} \mid \theta, \rho).
\end{align}

\subsection{Marginal Distributions and Identifiability}

The marginal distribution of each off-diagonal edge is
\begin{align}
\label{eq:marginal_rv}
A_{ij} \mid \delta_i, \gamma_j, \lambda
  \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\delta_i + \gamma_j,\; \lambda^{-1}\right),
\end{align}
where $\delta_i$ is a \emph{sender effect} measuring node $i$'s tendency
to exert influence, $\gamma_j$ is a \emph{receiver effect} measuring
node $j$'s tendency to be influenced, and $\lambda > 0$ is a precision
parameter shared across edges \citep{hoff2005}.

The additive mean structure $\delta_i + \gamma_j$ is not identified without
a constraint.  We set $\delta_1 = 0$, so all sender effects are interpreted
relative to node~1.  Under this normalisation, $\gamma_j$ equals the
expected weight of edges directed into node $j$ from node~1, and $\delta_i$
measures the excess sending tendency of node $i \geq 2$ relative to the
reference.

\subsection{Frank Copula Density for a Dyad}

By Sklar's theorem \citep{sklar1959}, the joint distribution of $(A_{ij},
A_{ji})$ is expressed via a copula binding their marginal CDFs:
\begin{align}
p(A_{ij}, A_{ji} \mid \theta, \rho)
  = c_\rho\!\left(F_{ij}(A_{ij}),\; F_{ji}(A_{ji})\right)
    \cdot f_{ij}(A_{ij}) \cdot f_{ji}(A_{ji}),
\end{align}
where $F_{ij}(a) = \Phi\!\left((a - \delta_i - \gamma_j)\sqrt{\lambda}\right)$,
$\Phi$ and $\phi$ are the standard normal CDF and density, and
$c_\rho(u,v) = \partial^2 C_\rho / \partial u\,\partial v$ is the Frank
copula density
\begin{align}
\label{eq:frank_density_rv}
c_\rho(u,v)
  = \frac{\rho(1 - e^{-\rho})\, e^{-\rho(u+v)}}
         {\left[1 - e^{-\rho} - (1-e^{-\rho u})(1-e^{-\rho v})\right]^2},
\qquad \rho \in \mathbb{R},
\end{align}
The log-likelihood contribution of dyad $\{i,j\}$ is therefore
\begin{align}
\label{eq:dyad_loglik_rv}
\log p(A_{ij}, A_{ji} \mid \theta, \rho)
  = \log c_\rho\!\left(\Phi(z_{ij}),\, \Phi(z_{ji})\right)
  + \log\phi(z_{ij}) + \log\phi(z_{ji}) + \log\lambda,
\end{align}
where $z_{ij} = (A_{ij} - \delta_i - \gamma_j)\sqrt{\lambda}$.

In particular, $\rho$ is the only parameter governing within-dyad
dependence; all remaining parameters affect only the marginal distributions.
The dependence parameter $\rho \in \mathbb{R}$ governs \emph{reciprocity}:
when $\rho > 0$ the edges $A_{ij}$ and $A_{ji}$ are positively associated;
when $\rho < 0$ they are negatively associated, implying a hierarchical or
asymmetric regulation structure; and $\rho = 0$ corresponds to independence
(the Frank copula reduces to the independence copula in the limit
$\rho \to 0$).  Figure~\ref{fig:rho_illustration} illustrates these three
regimes in the uniform (PIT) space after marginal structure is removed.
Section~\ref{sec:theory} formalises these properties.

The choice of copula family is guided by the empirical dependence structure
of the data (Figure~\ref{fig:copula_comparison}).  The brain dyad pairs
exhibit symmetric anti-diagonal concentration without evidence of tail
asymmetry.  This rules out asymmetric Archimedean families such as Clayton
and Gumbel, which place excess mass in one tail corner of the unit square
(Figure~\ref{fig:copula_comparison}, right column).  Among symmetric
copulas, the Gaussian copula imposes an elliptical dependence structure
that concentrates mass along linear contours; the Frank copula, by contrast,
produces a sharper, non-elliptical anti-diagonal concentration that better
matches the observed pattern (Figure~\ref{fig:copula_comparison}, center versus
left column).

The Frank family is therefore the simplest model that matches the observed
symmetric anti-diagonal dependence structure.  Beyond the empirical fit,
it has three additional theoretical properties that make it natural here.
First, it is the unique Archimedean family with radial symmetry
($C_\rho(u,v) = C_\rho(v,u)$), appropriate for an undirected dyad where
the roles of $A_{ij}$ and $A_{ji}$ are symmetric.  Second, $\rho$ is
defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$, spanning the full range from perfect
negative to perfect positive dependence with independence at $\rho = 0$,
natural for reciprocity which can take either sign.  Third, the one-to-one
correspondence between $\rho$ and Kendall's $\tau$ via the Debye function
(Theorem~\ref{thm:tau}) makes the parameter directly interpretable as a
rank-correlation measure of reciprocity, invariant to marginal
transformations.  Sensitivity to copula choice is discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:discuss}.

\subsection{Bayesian Inference}

We adopt a Bayesian framework with the following hierarchical prior:
\begin{align}
\rho_0,\, \delta_0,\, \gamma_0 &\;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(0, 4), \\
\rho^{(g)} \mid \rho_0, \sigma_\rho
  &\;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(\rho_0,\, \sigma_\rho^2), \\
\delta_i \mid \delta_0, \sigma_\delta
  &\;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(\delta_0,\, \sigma_\delta^2),
  \quad i = 2,\ldots,N, \\
\gamma_j \mid \gamma_0, \sigma_\gamma
  &\;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(\gamma_0,\, \sigma_\gamma^2),
  \quad j = 1,\ldots,N, \\
\lambda &\;\sim\; \mathrm{Gamma}(5,\, 4), \\
\sigma_\rho,\, \sigma_\delta,\, \sigma_\gamma
  &\;\sim\; \mathrm{Gamma}(0.1,\, 10).
\end{align}
The hyper-means $(\rho_0, \delta_0, \gamma_0)$ pool information across
groups and nodes respectively; the hyper-variances control the degree of
shrinkage.  Posterior samples are drawn via the No-U-Turn Sampler
\citep[NUTS;][]{hoffman2014} as implemented in NumPyro \citep{phan2019} with
JAX \citep{jax2018} for hardware-accelerated automatic differentiation.

\paragraph{MAP versus full posterior sampling.}
Maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation with a Laplace approximation is a
natural fast alternative and provides useful diagnostics: the MAP point
estimate is quick to compute, easy to cross-validate, and sufficient for
the Kendall's $\tau$ interpretation.  We use full MCMC rather than MAP for
three reasons specific to this application.  First, $\rho$ is the primary
inferential target and its posterior can be non-symmetric at moderate
$|\rho|$, making a diagonal Hessian approximation unreliable.  Second, the
hierarchical prior induces posterior dependence among group-level parameters
that a Laplace approximation around the MAP does not capture.  Third, full
MCMC gives ranking probabilities directly: the posterior probability
$\Pr(\rho^{(g_1)} < \rho^{(g_2)} < \rho^{(g_3)} \mid \mathrm{data})$ is
the most honest summary of the ordering claim and requires no additional
calculation beyond counting posterior draws.  We note that MAP estimates
are consistent with the posterior means reported in
Section~\ref{sec:brain_app}, supporting approximate unimodality.

\section{Theoretical Properties of the CGRG}
\label{sec:theory}

This section derives several properties that clarify the interpretation and
estimability of $\rho$.  The main results establish its margin-invariant rank
interpretation (Theorem~\ref{thm:tau}), identifiability for $N \geq 3$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:ident}), and $\sqrt{S}$-consistency of the MLE under a
simplified parametric model (Theorem~\ref{thm:mle}).  An entropy decomposition
is deferred to Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_entropy}.

\subsection{Reciprocity and Rank Correlation}

\begin{theorem}[Kendall's $\tau$ and Margin-Invariant Reciprocity]
\label{thm:tau}
Let $(A_{ij}, A_{ji})$ follow a CGRG with Frank copula parameter $\rho$ and
any continuous marginals.  Define the first-order Debye function
$D_1(\rho) = \rho^{-1}\int_0^\rho t(e^t - 1)^{-1}\,dt$, extended by
continuity to $D_1(0) = 1$.  Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\rm (a)] Kendall's $\tau$ satisfies
  $\tau(\rho) = 1 - 4\rho^{-1}[1 - D_1(\rho)]$.
\item[\rm (b)] $\tau(\rho)$ is strictly increasing in $\rho$ on
  $\mathbb{R}$, with $\tau(0) = 0$, $\tau(\rho) \to +1$ as
  $\rho \to +\infty$, and $\tau(\rho) \to -1$ as $\rho \to -\infty$.
\item[\rm (c)] $\operatorname{sign}(\tau(\rho)) = \operatorname{sign}(\rho)$.
\item[\rm (d)] $\tau(\rho)$ depends only on $\rho$, not on
  $\delta_i, \gamma_j$, or $\lambda$.  Therefore $\rho$ provides a
  \emph{margin-invariant} parametrization of reciprocity: its rank-correlation
  interpretation is unchanged by strictly increasing transformations of the
  marginals.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Part (a).} For any copula $C$, Kendall's $\tau$ satisfies
$\tau = 4\,\mathbb{E}_{(U,V) \sim C}[C(U,V)] - 1$ \citep[Theorem
5.1.3]{nelsen}.  Evaluating the expectation for the Frank family yields
part (a); see \citet[Section~5.3]{joe}.

\emph{Part (b).} Differentiating $\tau(\rho)$ and using
$D_1'(\rho) = \rho^{-1}[\rho(e^\rho-1)^{-1} - D_1(\rho)]$ gives
\[
\tau'(\rho)
  = \frac{4}{\rho^2}\!\left[1 - \frac{\rho}{e^\rho - 1}\right].
\]
Since $e^\rho - 1 > \rho$ for $\rho > 0$ and $e^\rho - 1 < \rho$ for
$\rho < 0$ (both by strict convexity of $e^\rho$), we have
$\tau'(\rho) > 0$ for all $\rho \neq 0$.  The limiting values follow
from $D_1(\rho) \to 0$ as $\rho \to +\infty$ and $D_1(\rho) \to 1$ as
$\rho \to 0$.

\emph{Part (c).} Follows from $\tau(0) = 0$ and strict monotonicity.

\emph{Part (d).} Kendall's $\tau$ is invariant under strictly increasing
marginal transformations \citep[Theorem~5.1.9]{nelsen}.  Since normal CDFs
are strictly increasing, the rank correlation depends only on $C_\rho$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Identifiability}

Theorem~\ref{thm:ident} applies to the simplified model of this section:
Gaussian marginals with fixed parameters $(\delta_i, \gamma_j, \lambda)$,
dyadic Frank copula with parameter $\rho$, and $S \geq 1$ i.i.d.\ replicated
networks.  Formal identifiability of the full hierarchical model in
Section~\ref{sec:cgrg_brain} involves additional considerations
(shared hyperparameters and group-specific precision parameters) that
are not addressed here; extending the argument to that setting is of
theoretical interest and left for future work.

\begin{theorem}[Identifiability]
\label{thm:ident}
Under the constraint $\delta_1 = 0$, the parameter vector
\[
\theta = (\delta_2, \ldots, \delta_N,\; \gamma_1, \ldots, \gamma_N,\;
          \lambda,\; \rho)
\]
is identifiable from the joint distribution of
$\{(A_{ij}, A_{ji}) : 1 \leq i < j \leq N\}$ whenever $N \geq 3$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Denote the marginal mean $\mu_{ij} = \mathbb{E}[A_{ij}] = \delta_i +
\gamma_j$ and the common marginal variance $\sigma^2 = \lambda^{-1}$.

\emph{Step 1: $\lambda$.}
Every marginal is $\mathcal{N}(\mu_{ij}, \lambda^{-1})$, so
$\lambda^{-1} = \mathrm{Var}(A_{ij})$ for any $i \neq j$.
Hence $\lambda$ is identified.

\emph{Step 2: Receiver effects $\gamma_j$ for $j \geq 2$.}
Because $\delta_1 = 0$, we have $\mu_{1j} = \gamma_j$ for all $j \geq 2$.
Thus $\gamma_j = \mu_{1j}$ is identified directly from the marginal mean
of $A_{1j}$.

\emph{Step 3: Sender effects $\delta_i$ for $i \geq 2$ and $\gamma_1$.}
For $i \geq 2$, we have $\mu_{i1} = \delta_i + \gamma_1$, so once
$\gamma_1$ is known, $\delta_i = \mu_{i1} - \gamma_1$.  It remains to
identify $\gamma_1$.  For any $i \geq 2$ and $j \geq 2$ with $j \neq 1$,
\[
\delta_i = \mu_{ij} - \gamma_j = \mu_{ij} - \mu_{1j},
\]
using the expressions from Step~2.  Substituting back into
$\gamma_1 = \mu_{i1} - \delta_i$ gives
\[
\gamma_1 = \mu_{i1} - (\mu_{ij} - \mu_{1j}).
\]
This requires at least one pair $(i,j)$ with $i,j \geq 2$ and $i \neq j$,
which exists whenever $N \geq 3$.  (With $N = 2$ the only pair is $(2,1)$,
giving $\delta_2 + \gamma_1 = \mu_{21}$, a single equation in two unknowns.)

\emph{Step 4: $\rho$ from the copula.}
With $\lambda$ and the mean structure identified, the marginal CDFs
$F_{ij}(a) = \Phi((a - \mu_{ij})\sqrt{\lambda})$ are fully known.
The joint CDF $H_{ij}(a,b) = C_\rho(F_{ij}(a), F_{ji}(b))$ determines
$C_\rho$ via $C_\rho(u,v) = H_{ij}(F_{ij}^{-1}(u), F_{ji}^{-1}(v))$.
By Sklar's theorem, the copula of a bivariate distribution with continuous
marginals is unique.  Since $\rho \mapsto C_\rho$ is injective for the
Frank family (strict monotonicity of $\tau(\rho)$ in
Theorem~\ref{thm:tau}(b) implies $C_\rho \neq C_{\rho'}$ for
$\rho \neq \rho'$), $\rho$ is uniquely determined.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Asymptotic Inference}

The asymptotic result below applies to the simplified CGRG of this section
(fixed $N$, shared $\rho$, i.i.d.\ replicates), not to the full hierarchical
model of Section~\ref{sec:cgrg_brain}.  The natural growing-sample regime for
replicated network data is fixed number of nodes $N$ and growing number of
replicated networks $S$.

\begin{theorem}[$\sqrt{S}$-Consistency of the MLE for $\rho$]
\label{thm:mle}
Fix $N \geq 3$ and let $S \to \infty$.  Assume: (i) the $S$ networks are
independent replicates from the CGRG model with true parameter
$\rho_0 \in \mathbb{R}$; (ii) all $\delta_i$ and $\gamma_j$ are
known, so $\rho$ is the only unknown; (iii) the marginal parameters are such
that the probability-integral-transformed dyad values $(U_s^{ij},V_s^{ij})$
are i.i.d.\ $c_{\rho_0}$ for each pair $i < j$.  Define the Fisher information
for $\rho$ from the $m = N(N-1)/2$ dyads of a single replicate:
\[
\mathcal{I}(\rho)
  = m \cdot \mathbb{E}_{c_\rho}\!\left[
    \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\log c_\rho(U,V)\right)^{\!2}
    \right].
\]
Then $\mathcal{I}(\rho) \in (0,\infty)$ for all $\rho \neq 0$, and the
maximum likelihood estimator $\hat\rho_S$ satisfies
\[
\sqrt{S}\,(\hat\rho_S - \rho_0)
  \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\;
    \mathcal{I}(\rho_0)^{-1}\right)
\quad \text{as } S \to \infty.
\]
The independence case $\rho_0 = 0$ requires separate local analysis
and is not covered by this result.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The Frank log-copula density is smooth in $\rho$ on $(0,1)^2 \times
(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})$, with bounded score, giving
$\mathcal{I}(\rho) < \infty$.  Positivity follows from strict monotonicity
of $\tau(\rho)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:tau}(b)).  Asymptotic normality then
follows from the standard M-estimator CLT \citep[Theorem~5.41]{vandervaart98}
applied to the i.i.d.\ sum $\mathcal{L}_S(\rho) =
\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{i<j}\ell_\rho(U_s^{ij},V_s^{ij})$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Theorem~\ref{thm:mle} fixes the marginal parameters and treats $\rho$ as
the sole unknown; it does not cover the full hierarchical model or joint
estimation.  Its value is to establish the $\sqrt{S}$ information-scaling
for replicated-network data.  Joint asymptotics under simultaneous estimation
would require additional profile likelihood analysis and is left for future
work.
\end{remark}

\section{Hierarchical CGRG for Replicated Brain Networks}
\label{sec:cgrg_brain}

\subsection{Data and Network Construction}

We apply the model to $S = 112$ resting-state fMRI-derived effective
connectivity networks from \citet{nie2020}.  Each network is a $4 \times 4$
matrix $A^{(s)}$ of connectivity strengths estimated by spectral dynamic
causal modelling \citep{friston2014} for the four canonical nodes of the
default mode network (DMN): medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC, node~1),
posterior cingulate cortex (PCC, node~2), left inferior parietal lobule
(LIPL, node~3), and right inferior parietal lobule (RIPL, node~4).
These four regions are the core hubs of the DMN and are known to show
altered effective connectivity in Alzheimer's disease
\citep{dmn2016, huang2022, monash2024}.
Participants are classified as Alzheimer's disease (AD, $S_{\mathrm{AD}} = 12$),
mild cognitive impairment (MCI, $S_{\mathrm{MCI}} = 63$), or cognitively normal
(NL, $S_{\mathrm{NL}} = 37$) based on ADNI diagnostic criteria \citep{ma2019},
with group labels $g_s \in \{\mathrm{AD}, \mathrm{MCI}, \mathrm{NL}\}$.
Throughout this section $N = 4$ denotes the number of DMN regions (nodes) and
$S = 112$ denotes the total number of participants (replicated networks).

\subsection{Multi-Group CGRG Model}

The network edges fall into two types.  \emph{Self-connections}
$A^{(s)}_{ii}$ capture the intrinsic decay of each region.  \emph{Off-diagonal
edges} $A^{(s)}_{ij}$ ($i \neq j$) represent directed regulatory strengths and
are the primary object of interest.

Following Section~\ref{sec:realvalued}, the marginal distribution of each
off-diagonal edge is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:brain_marginal}
A^{(s)}_{ij} \mid \delta_i^{(g_s)}, \gamma_j^{(g_s)}, \lambda^{(g_s)}
  \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\delta_i^{(g_s)} + \gamma_j^{(g_s)},\;
    (\lambda^{(g_s)})^{-1}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\delta_i^{(g)}$ is the sender effect of node $i$ in group $g$,
measuring the tendency of region $i$ to exert regulatory influence, and
$\gamma_j^{(g)}$ is the receiver effect of node $j$, measuring the
tendency of region $j$ to be regulated.  The precision $\lambda^{(g)}$ is
group-specific.  We set $\delta_1^{(g)} = 0$ for all groups.  Self-connections
are modeled marginally as $A^{(s)}_{ii} \sim \mathcal{N}(\delta_i^{(g_s)} +
\gamma_i^{(g_s)}, (\lambda^{(g_s)})^{-1})$; they do not enter the copula.

Within each group $g$, the two directed edges of dyad $\{i,j\}$ are
joined by a Frank copula with group-specific parameter $\rho^{(g)}$:
\[
p(A^{(s)}_{ij}, A^{(s)}_{ji} \mid \rho^{(g_s)}, \ldots)
  = c_{\rho^{(g_s)}}\!\left(F^{(g_s)}_{ij}(A^{(s)}_{ij}),\,
    F^{(g_s)}_{ji}(A^{(s)}_{ji})\right)
    \cdot f^{(g_s)}_{ij}(A^{(s)}_{ij}) \cdot f^{(g_s)}_{ji}(A^{(s)}_{ji}),
\]
where $F^{(g)}_{ij}$ and $f^{(g)}_{ij}$ are the CDF and density of
\eqref{eq:brain_marginal}.  The group log-likelihood is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:brain_loglik}
\log p(\{A^{(s)}\}_{g_s=g} \mid \theta^{(g)}, \rho^{(g)})
  = \sum_{s:\,g_s=g}\!\left[
      \sum_{i=1}^4 \log p(A^{(s)}_{ii})
      + \sum_{i < j} \log p(A^{(s)}_{ij}, A^{(s)}_{ji})
    \right].
\end{equation}

\subsection{Hierarchical Prior}

The sender effects, receiver effects, and copula parameters are linked
across groups via shared hyperparameters, reflecting the belief that the
functional roles of the four DMN nodes are similar across disease states:
\begin{align}
\rho_0,\, \delta_0,\, \gamma_0 &\;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(0, 4),
  \label{eq:hyperprior} \\
\rho^{(g)} \mid \rho_0, \sigma_\rho
  &\;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(\rho_0,\, \sigma_\rho^2),
  \quad g = 1,2,3, \\
\delta_i^{(g)} \mid \delta_0, \sigma_\delta
  &\;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(\delta_0,\, \sigma_\delta^2),
  \quad i = 2,3,4,\; g = 1,2,3, \\
\gamma_j^{(g)} \mid \gamma_0, \sigma_\gamma
  &\;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(\gamma_0,\, \sigma_\gamma^2),
  \quad j = 1,\ldots,4,\; g = 1,2,3, \\
\lambda^{(g)} &\;\sim\; \mathrm{Gamma}(5,\, 4),
  \quad g = 1,2,3, \\
\sigma_\rho,\, \sigma_\delta,\, \sigma_\gamma
  &\;\sim\; \mathrm{Gamma}(0.1,\, 10).
  \label{eq:hyperprior_end}
\end{align}
The hyper-means $(\rho_0, \delta_0, \gamma_0)$ pool information across the
three groups; the hyper-standard-deviations $(\sigma_\rho, \sigma_\delta,
\sigma_\gamma)$ control the degree of shrinkage.

\subsection{Posterior Computation}

Posterior samples are drawn via the No-U-Turn Sampler
\citep[NUTS;][]{hoffman2014} as implemented in NumPyro \citep{phan2019} with
JAX \citep{jax2018} for hardware-accelerated automatic differentiation.
We run 1,000 warmup iterations followed by 3,000 post-warmup draws per chain,
with two independent chains initialised from dispersed starting values,
sampling all 42 scalar parameters jointly.  The posterior probability of the
strict group ordering,
$\Pr(\rho^{(\mathrm{NL})} < \rho^{(\mathrm{MCI})} < \rho^{(\mathrm{AD})}
\mid \mathrm{data}) = 0.20$, is computed directly by counting the fraction of
retained draws satisfying the inequality; this single number summarises
the confidence in the ordering claim without requiring any additional
analytical approximation.  Convergence is assessed via the Gelman--Rubin
$\hat{R}$ statistic and the bulk effective sample size (ESS): $\hat{R} < 1.05$
and ESS $> 400$ for all $\rho^{(g)}$ and $\lambda^{(g)}$ parameters.
The hierarchical variance parameters $\sigma_\delta$ and $\sigma_\gamma$
exhibit lower ESS ($\approx 50$--$100$) consistent with near-boundary
concentration in the half-normal prior, but do not affect the primary
inferential targets.

\section{Relationship to the Multivariate Normal}
\label{sec:compare}

A natural alternative to the CGRG is a bivariate normal model for each dyad,
with common marginal variance $\lambda^{-1}$ and a dyad-specific correlation
$r_{ij}$:
\[
\begin{pmatrix} A_{ij} \\ A_{ji} \end{pmatrix}
\sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(
  \begin{pmatrix} \delta_i + \gamma_j \\ \delta_j + \gamma_i \end{pmatrix},\;
  \frac{1}{\lambda}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & r_{ij} \\ r_{ij} & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\right).
\]
The CGRG replaces this Gaussian joint distribution with a Frank-copula joint
distribution sharing the same marginals.  Three differences are worth noting.

First, the Frank copula is not a Gaussian copula, so the two models differ in
their dependence structure even when Kendall's $\tau$ is matched via
$r \approx \sin(\pi\tau/2)$.  The Frank copula has symmetric, lighter tails
than the Gaussian; for effective connectivity data, this means the CGRG does
not force a specific tail dependence pattern that may not hold.

Second, the CGRG uses a single scalar $\rho$ across all dyads within a group,
whereas the dyad-specific bivariate normal uses $N(N-1)/2$ correlation
parameters per group.  This parsimony is the primary practical advantage in
the replicated-network setting: with small $S_g$, a single $\rho$ is
estimable where dyad-specific correlations would require pooling assumptions
or regularization.

Third, the margin-invariance of $\rho$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:tau}(d)) has no
counterpart in the bivariate normal: Pearson correlation depends on the marginal
distributions, whereas Kendall's $\tau$ and the Frank copula parameter do not.
This makes $\rho$ directly comparable across groups even when sender/receiver
effects differ.

\section{Simulation Study}
\label{sec:sim}

We assess parameter recovery under two scenarios, each with $N = 5$ nodes and
$S = 100$ replicated networks per group.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scenario~1}: $\rho^{(1)} = -7$ (strong negative reciprocity),
  $\delta^{(1)} = (0, 2, -2, 0.25, -0.5)$,
  $\gamma^{(1)} = (15, 0.5, -2, 2, -1)$, $\lambda^{(1)} = 1$.
\item \textbf{Scenario~2}: $\rho^{(2)} = -3$ (moderate negative reciprocity),
  $\delta^{(2)} = (0, 0.33, 8, -5, 9)$,
  $\gamma^{(2)} = (3, -12, 0, 4, -3)$, $\lambda^{(2)} = 1$.
\end{itemize}

Data were generated using the Frank copula with Gaussian marginals via the
conditional quantile method: for each replicate and each dyad $\{i,j\}$,
a pair $(u,v)$ was drawn from the Frank copula $C_\rho$ by sampling $u \sim
\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ and $v = C_\rho^{-1}(v|u)$ via conditional inversion,
then transforming $(A_{ij}, A_{ji}) = (F_{ij}^{-1}(u), F_{ji}^{-1}(v))$ using
the Gaussian marginal quantile functions.  Table~\ref{tab:sim} summarises
posterior performance from a single simulated dataset per scenario; it
illustrates parameter recovery under the model but does not constitute a
repeated-simulation coverage study.  The copula parameter $\rho$ is recovered
less precisely than $\gamma$ (larger SD relative to the scale of the
parameter), as expected: $\rho$ is identified only through higher-order joint
structure, while $\gamma_j$ is identified from marginal means.
Figures~\ref{fig:sim1} and \ref{fig:sim2} display the marginal posteriors of
$\rho$ under the two scenarios.

Additional simulations with $\rho = 0$ ($N = 5$, $S = 100$) confirm that the
posterior concentrates near zero, verifying that the model does not spuriously
detect reciprocity when none is present.  This null-signal scenario mirrors
the brain application result.  A systematic repeated-simulation study
reporting interval coverage and RMSE across many datasets is warranted and
left for future work.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Reciprocity}

The CGRG model provides a direct test of dyadic dependence via $\rho$.
Hierarchical (non-reciprocal) organisation implies that each region
specialises as either a net sender or a net receiver, and would be
characterised by negative $\rho$ in the Frank copula model
(Theorem~\ref{thm:tau}).  We test for this structure below.

Figure~\ref{fig:reciprocity_cgrg} shows posterior marginals of $\rho^{(g)}$
for the three groups.  Posterior means and 95\% credible intervals are
reported in Table~\ref{tab:rho_summary}.  All three posteriors are tightly
concentrated around $\rho \approx -3.75$, well below zero, indicating strong
negative dyadic dependence: participants with a stronger effective connection
$A_{ij}$ tend to have a weaker reverse connection $A_{ji}$, consistent with a
hierarchical (non-reciprocal) regulatory structure in the DMN
(Theorem~\ref{thm:tau}).  The posterior for $\rho$ corresponds to a
Kendall's $\tau$ of approximately $-0.43$ via
$\tau(\rho) = 1 - 4\rho^{-1}[1-D_1(\rho)]$, matching the sample Kendall's
$\tau$ of $-0.37$ to $-0.55$ observed across dyads.

The AD group, with only 12 participants ($n_{\mathrm{AD}} = 72$ dyad
observations), shows the widest posterior ($[-4.34,\,-2.91]$), as expected
from the smaller sample, but it is still clearly concentrated below zero.
All three groups have nearly identical posterior means for $\rho$; the
posterior probability of the strict ordering
$\Pr(\rho^{(\mathrm{NL})} < \rho^{(\mathrm{MCI})} < \rho^{(\mathrm{AD})}
\mid \mathrm{data}) = 0.20$, close to the uniform chance level of
$1/6 \approx 0.17$, indicating that disease state does not substantially
alter the degree of hierarchical reciprocity.

\subsection{Predictive Validation}

Figure~\ref{fig:copula_gain} assesses how much the Frank copula improves
predictive fit relative to an independence baseline ($\rho = 0$, identical
marginals).  For each participant $s$ in group $g$ we compute the average
copula log-density across the six DMN dyads,
\[
\mathrm{gain}_s
  \;=\; \frac{1}{6}\sum_{i<j}
        \log c_{\hat\rho^{(g_s)}}(u_{ij}^{(s)},\, u_{ji}^{(s)}),
\]
where $(u_{ij}^{(s)}, u_{ji}^{(s)})$ are the probability-integral-transformed
edges under posterior mean parameters.  Under the independence copula
$\log c_0 = 0$, so $\mathrm{gain}_s > 0$ means the copula model assigns
higher density to the observed pair than independence does.  Because
$\hat\rho \approx -3.75$ and the data show anti-diagonal structure
($u_{ij}$ high $\Leftrightarrow$ $u_{ji}$ low), the Frank copula
concentrates mass precisely in the anti-diagonal region occupied by the
observations, yielding consistently positive gains across all 112
participants and all three groups.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discuss}

This paper has developed a dyadic Frank-copula model for directed real-valued
networks, combining an additive sender-receiver mean structure with a single
margin-invariant reciprocity parameter $\rho$.  Section~\ref{sec:theory}
derives that $\rho$ admits a closed-form interpretation via Kendall's $\tau$,
is identifiable for $N \geq 3$, and is $\sqrt{S}$-consistently estimable
in a simplified parametric benchmark; the entropy decomposition is deferred
to Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_entropy}.

The brain connectivity application reveals a strong and consistent signal
in this cohort.
Posterior estimates of $\rho$ are concentrated around $-3.75$ for all three
diagnostic groups, with 95\% credible intervals entirely below zero.  A
Kendall's $\tau$ of approximately $-0.43$ implies that, within each dyad,
participants with stronger forward connectivity $A_{ij}$ systematically have
weaker reverse connectivity $A_{ji}$.  This pattern is consistent with a
hierarchical (non-reciprocal) regulatory signature in the DMN under this
modelling framework.
The three groups share nearly the same $\rho$: the posterior probability of
the strict ordering $\rho^{(\mathrm{NL})} < \rho^{(\mathrm{MCI})} <
\rho^{(\mathrm{AD})}$ is $0.20$, close to chance, indicating that the
degree of hierarchical organisation does not change substantially across
disease states in this cohort.  The lack of a group difference may reflect
genuine invariance of the hierarchical DMN structure across Alzheimer's
progression, or insufficient statistical power to resolve a small
between-group shift in $\rho$ with these sample sizes (particularly
$S_{\mathrm{AD}} = 12$).  Node-level receiver and sender effects
in Table~\ref{tab:node_effects} and Figure~\ref{fig:heatmaps} show positive receiver
effects across all groups, with LIPL and RIPL showing the largest positive
values, consistent with the known role of parietal regions as network hubs
\citep{huang2022}.  Posterior predictive checks (Figure~\ref{fig:ppc_tau})
confirm that the model reproduces the observed dyadic Kendall's $\tau$
values, and the copula gain analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:copula_gain}) shows
that the Frank copula model assigns substantially higher density to the
observed dyad pairs than the independence baseline for all 112 participants.

Several extensions are worth pursuing.  First, a proper repeated-simulation study
reporting interval coverage and RMSE over many datasets would replace the
single-dataset illustration of Section~\ref{sec:sim}.  Fourth, the dyadic
partition can be replaced by larger blocks via vine copula constructions
\citep{czado2025}.  Fifth, longitudinal brain networks require a dynamic
version; the spectral DCM framework of \citet{nie2020} is one starting point.
Sixth, a joint asymptotics result would require additional profile likelihood
analysis.  Seventh, extending identifiability to the full hierarchical model
is of theoretical interest.

\paragraph{External validation datasets.}
The current brain application is limited to 112 participants, with only 12 in
the AD group.  Several publicly available resting-state fMRI datasets offer
promising validation targets.  For disease-relevant replication, the
Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) and the Open Access Series
of Imaging Studies (OASIS-3) both include larger AD and MCI cohorts.  For
method stress-testing, the Human Connectome Project (HCP) Young Adult dataset
provides 1,206 healthy participants with high-quality effective connectivity
derivable from preprocessed timeseries.  The COBRE dataset (72 schizophrenia
patients, 75 controls) offers a convenient case-control replication in a
different clinical domain.  Validation on one of these datasets would
substantially strengthen confidence in the model's generalizability.

\paragraph{Relationship to deep learning methods.}
Graph neural networks (GNNs) and related deep learning approaches have been
applied to larger neuroimaging datasets for diagnostic classification
\citep{huang2022}.  The CGRG is not a competitor to GNNs for prediction tasks
but targets a different goal: estimating an interpretable, low-dimensional
dependence parameter for hypothesis testing in a replicated-network setting.
With $N = 4$ nodes and $S = 112$ participants, the data are far below the
scale at which GNN approaches are effective; a neural model would overfit and
obscure the parameter of scientific interest.  GNN-based approaches are
appropriate when the goal is classification with large node sets and many
subjects, not when the goal is estimating a bivariate dependence structure
interpretable via Kendall's $\tau$.  Future work combining subject-level
graph encoders with copula-based dependence parameters for larger ROI-level
networks remains an interesting direction.

\section{Entropy Decomposition}
\label{sec:appendix_entropy}

\begin{theorem}[Entropy Decomposition]
\label{thm:entropy}
Let $h_C(\rho) = -\int_0^1\!\int_0^1 c_\rho(u,v)\log c_\rho(u,v)\,du\,dv$
denote the \emph{copula entropy} of the Frank copula, with $h_C(0) = 0$.
Then: (a) $h_C(\rho) \leq 0$ with equality iff $\rho = 0$, with
$h_C(\rho) = -D_{\mathrm{KL}}(c_\rho \| c_0)$; (b) $h_C(\rho)$ is
decreasing in $|\rho|$; (c) $H(A_{ij}, A_{ji}) = H(A_{ij}) + H(A_{ji}) +
h_C(\rho)$; and (d) $I(A_{ij};\,A_{ji}) = -h_C(\rho) \geq 0$.
\end{theorem}

The proof is standard: part~(a) follows from KL non-negativity, parts~(b)--(c)
from the concordance ordering of the Frank family and the probability integral
transform, and part~(d) from the definition of mutual information.

\begin{corollary}[Group Entropy Differences Under Equal Marginal Precision]
\label{cor:group_entropy}
Under the CGRG model with $\lambda^{(g)} = \lambda$ for all $g$,
$H_{\mathrm{net}}^{(g)} - H_{\mathrm{net}}^{(g')}
  = \frac{N(N-1)}{2}[h_C(\rho^{(g)}) - h_C(\rho^{(g')})]$.
The copula term isolates the contribution of dyadic dependence when marginal
precision is shared across groups.
\end{corollary}

In the brain application the posterior estimates $\rho^{(g)} \approx -3.75$
yield $h_C(\rho^{(g)}) < 0$, meaning dyadic dependence reduces joint entropy
relative to independence.  The group-specific precision differences
$\lambda^{(g)}$ contribute additionally to between-group entropy variation,
and the two contributions can be separated via this decomposition.
Figure~\ref{fig:entropy_decomp} illustrates both components from the posterior.
